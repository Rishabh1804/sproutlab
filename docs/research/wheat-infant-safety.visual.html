The three-way split — keep them apart

Wheat is the one infant food where three distinct medical concepts collide under one word. Conflating them produces both needless fear and genuine error. Keep them labelled — this is the spine of the whole brief. consensus

Three things wear the word "wheat" the centerpiece

(1) THIS record
Wheat allergy
IgE food allergy to wheat proteins. Fast — hives, swelling, vomiting, eczema flare, at the severe end anaphylaxis. The introduce-early target. Commonly outgrown by early childhood.
(2) NOT an allergy
Celiac disease
Chronic autoimmune reaction to gluten (wheat/barley/rye). NOT anaphylactic, NOT an emergency. Chronic — poor growth, chronic GI, failure to thrive. Needs medical diagnosis (serology + specialist).
(3) A celiac question
Gluten-timing
When to introduce gluten — debated in the celiac literature, separate from allergy. ESPGHAN: anytime 4–12 months; timing shifts when celiac presents, not whether.

All chart values in text: (1) wheat allergy = IgE, fast, outgrown, the introduce-early target. (2) celiac = chronic autoimmune to gluten, not anaphylactic, not an emergency, medically diagnosed. (3) gluten-timing = ESPGHAN's 4–12-month celiac-prevention question.

⚑ Why wheat sits in both conversations. Wheat contains gluten, so it's simultaneously an allergen (wheat protein → IgE allergy) and the vehicle for gluten (→ celiac). A parent hearing "introduce wheat early" and "be careful about gluten timing" is hearing two different bodies answering two different questions. Answer each in its own lane; never let one borrow the other's authority.

Axis 1 — Introduce ~6 months, don't delay

Wheat is named among the common infant allergens and handled like the others: offer early, don't delay, keep it in the diet. The NHS explicitly lists "foods containing gluten, including wheat, barley and rye" among the allergenic foods to introduce from around 6 months, one at a time. Delaying allergens does not prevent allergy. AAP·NHS·ASCIA·NIAID

The honest limit — EAT found NO wheat benefit NEJM 2016, verified

The EAT trial (Perkin/Lack, NEJM 2016) randomised general-population breastfed infants to early introduction of six allergens (incl. wheat) vs standard. Its abstract states, verbatim:

"…there were no significant effects with respect to milk, sesame, fish, or wheat." PubMed 26943128

The trial's prevention benefit was confined to peanut and egg (and only in the per-protocol, dose-achieving analysis). Early wheat was safe but did not prevent wheat allergy.

⚑ Tier-2, not Tier-1. Say "introduce wheat early — it's safe, don't delay" without saying "early wheat prevents wheat allergy." That second claim is unproven and EAT is the direct evidence against it. The introduce-early posture stands on safety + the don't-delay consensus, not on a proven prevention RCT the way peanut stands on LEAP.

The gluten-timing lane — ESPGHAN 2016 (a celiac document) PubMed 26815017

  • "Gluten may be introduced into the infant's diet anytime between 4 and 12 completed months of age."
  • Age of introduction does not change lifetime celiac incidence — earlier vs later only shifts when celiac presents, not whether it occurs.
  • Breastfeeding reversal: neither breastfeeding nor breastfeeding at the time of gluten introduction reduces celiac risk — explicitly reversing the 2008 advice.
  • "Consumption of large quantities of gluten should be avoided during the first weeks after gluten introduction and during infancy."
✓ Practical reconciliation at ~6 months. Both lanes agree wheat/gluten can begin around 6 months — the allergy lane says "introduce early, don't delay," and the celiac lane (ESPGHAN) says "anytime 4–12 months, avoid large quantities early." Compatible: introduce wheat around 6 months in ordinary food-sized amounts, not in a gluten-loading binge.

Reaction & introduction — know mild from severe

Mild–moderate IgE reaction (one body system). Itchy hives/welts, redness or rash, swelling (especially mouth/eyes), vomiting, GI upset/loose stool, eczema flare, sudden fussiness. → stop the food, monitor closely, contact your doctor; a clinician may advise an antihistamine.
Severe / anaphylaxis — act immediately. Difficulty breathing, wheeze/persistent cough/noisy breathing, swelling of throat/tongue/lips/face, hoarse cry, pale/floppy/lethargic or unusually sleepy, collapse. → use a prescribed adrenaline auto-injector and call emergency services (112 / 108 in India).

In a baby, sudden behaviour change (limp, can't hold head up) is a recognised severe sign. Two or more body systems involved = treat as anaphylaxis. Reactions appear minutes to ~2 hours after eating; anaphylaxis often within 5–30 min.

⚑ Celiac is explicitly NOT here. Celiac disease is chronic, not acute — it does not cause anaphylaxis and is not a 112/108 event. It presents over weeks/months (poor growth, chronic GI) and needs medical diagnosis, not emergency response. Don't let celiac symptoms ride the allergy-emergency rail, and don't let a parent worried about celiac think they're watching for anaphylaxis.
⚑ Aside — WDEIA (wheat-dependent exercise-induced anaphylaxis) is an adult/adolescent phenomenon, not relevant to infants. Mentioned only so it isn't mistaken for an infant wheat-allergy pattern. Do not surface in infant copy except to rule out.

Safe FORM — softened roti/cereal vs the choke

Soft, infant-friendly (SAFE)
good first forms
Soft wheat/multigrain cereal (thin porridge); well-cooked soft pasta; softened roti/chapati torn small & soaked in milk/dal/sabzi; suji (sooji/rava) / dalia cooked soft to a smooth mash.
Two wheat choke-traps (CHOKING)
toast it / bind it
Fresh soft bread balls up into a sticky clump → toast it or use firm strips. Loose cooked wheat/dalia/suji scatters → cook soft and mash or bind (porridge, with milk/dal) so it holds together.
✓ Form addresses choking, not allergy. Same principle as nuts — cooking/softening does not remove the allergy hazard or the celiac relevance. A baby with wheat-protein allergy reacts to soft well-cooked roti just as to bread. Form is the choking lane only. forms partly secondary, flagged

First-exposure timeline

Offer a soft form, one allergen at a time
Soft cereal/porridge or softened roti — in ordinary food-sized amounts (not a gluten-loading binge).
Watch ~2 hours
Reactions appear minutes to ~2 hours after eating; anaphylaxis often within 5–30 minutes.
If severe signs — emergency
Breathing trouble, face/throat swelling, floppiness, or two body systems → adrenaline + 112/108.
If tolerated — keep wheat in the diet
Wheat is an everyday Indian staple; ongoing offering is culturally trivial. Allergy commonly outgrown in early childhood.

Context — the Indian alias thicket

Wheat is already in the diet daily — roti, dalia, suji, sevai — so introduction is culturally trivial: softened roti/chapati, soft cooked suji/dalia, well-cooked pasta are near-ideal forms. The trap is the alias thicket: many names are wheat, and fresh Indian staples carry no allergen label — so caregiver alias-knowledge is the safeguard, not the package. alias list: High

Wheat hides under many names

Core flours & forms
  • atta (wholewheat), maida (refined)
  • semolina / suji / sooji / rava
  • broken wheat / dalia; sevai / vermicelli
  • durum, farina, couscous, bulgur
  • seitan (= wheat gluten); soy sauce (typically contains wheat)
Indian staples that ARE wheat
  • roti, chapati, paratha, naan, pav
  • upma (suji), halwa (suji/atta)
  • samosa & pakora batter/wrapper
  • rusks and biscuits; sevai/vermicelli dishes
Related wheat grains (often missed): spelt, kamut, einkorn, emmer, farro, triticale — all contain wheat/gluten and are not "wheat-free." FARE/FAACT

Myths to correct

Myth: "Delay wheat to prevent allergy." No — delaying does not prevent allergy; introduce around 6 months and don't hold it back. (Honest companion truth: early wheat also wasn't shown to prevent allergy — EAT was null — so the line is "introduce early because it's safe and delaying has no benefit," not "to prevent allergy.")
Myth: "Wheat allergy and gluten intolerance / celiac are the same." They are NOT. Fast IgE reaction to wheat protein vs chronic autoimmune reaction to gluten, diagnosed medically and never anaphylactic. Conflating causes false alarms and false reassurance.
Myth: "You must introduce gluten while breastfeeding to prevent celiac." Withdrawn. ESPGHAN 2016 reversed this; the old 2008 advice no longer stands. High-value to surface because it's stale official advice, not folklore — name the reversal explicitly.
