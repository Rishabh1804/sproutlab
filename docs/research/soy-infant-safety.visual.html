Introduce early — but prevention is NOT proven for soy

Mainstream guidance (AAP, NHS, ASCIA, NIAID): introduce the major allergens — soy among them — around 6 months alongside other solids, not before 4 months, and don't deliberately delay. Soy is one of the US "Big 9" major allergens. consensus

But the prevention evidence (LEAP/EAT) is for peanut and egg. In EAT (1,303 exclusively breastfed infants, 6 allergens from 3mo vs ~6mo), the per-protocol benefit was significant for peanut and egg only. Soy showed no significant prevention effect — nor did milk, sesame, or fish. NEJM 2016

⚑ The honest framing the model MUST use: introducing soy early is safe and appropriate, and there's no reason to delay it — but we cannot tell a parent that early soy prevents soy allergy. That claim is true for peanut/egg, false-by-evidence for soy. Tier-2 allergen: introduce-early because there's no reason not to and the diet benefits, NOT because an RCT proved prevention.

Why introduce at all, then?

Nutrition + no-reason-to-avoid, not proven prevention. Soy is a complete plant protein — especially valuable in a vegetarian household — and normal allergen exposure as part of a varied diet beats the downsides of unnecessary restriction.

Often outgrown — soy is not a lifelong sentence FARE · JACI (clinical)

Infant prevalence~0.4%

Soy allergy affects ~0.4% of infants and is commonly outgrown — many by ~age 3, the majority by age 10. It behaves more like milk/egg (often outgrown) than peanut/tree-nut (often lifelong). "Soy allergy is permanent" is a myth.

✓ The design crux. Mashing tofu/soybeans genuinely makes the food safe on the choking axis — but it does nothing for the allergy axis (IgE or FPIES), which is managed by introduction + watchfulness. Two axes, held separately.

The TWO emergency branches — a hives-centric card misses FPIES

Branch A — IgE allergy · FAST (minutes–~2h)
Mild (one body system): itchy hives/welts, redness, swelling (mouth/eyes), vomiting, sometimes loose stool, sudden fussiness. In infants reactions skew to skin + GI.
Branch A — anaphylaxis · act immediately
Trouble breathing / wheeze / persistent cough, throat/tongue/lip/face swelling, pale or blue, floppy/limp/lethargic, collapse. → adrenaline auto-injector if prescribed + call 112 (108 India; 999 UK; 911 US). Lie flat, or sit up if breathing is hard.
Branch B — FPIES · DELAYED (1–4h after eating)
Non-IgE reaction. Profuse, repetitive (projectile) vomiting, then lethargy, pallor, risk of dehydration; sometimes diarrhoea later. Often NO hives, NO swelling, NO breathing trouble — looks like a stomach bug. Standard allergy tests (skin-prick, sIgE) come back negative. Soy is a classic trigger.
Branch B — FPIES · urgent care even with no rash
Repetitive vomiting + a pale/floppy/lethargic baby a couple of hours after a soy feed → urgent medical care even without any rash; severe episodes can cause dehydration/shock and may need IV fluids. Tell the clinician what was eaten and when — the delay is the diagnostic clue.
⚑ The most important soy-specific safety insight. A hives-centric allergy card will miss FPIES entirely. The soy record needs a distinct "delayed vomiting + lethargy, no rash" branch that routes to urgent care, separate from the IgE/anaphylaxis branch. Classic FPIES triggers: cow's milk, soy, rice, oats. CHOP — clinical secondary

First-exposure approach

Start soy as soft/silken tofu or as a minor ingredient mashed into an already-tolerated dish (minor-ingredient-first matters for the CMPA cross-reactivity below), watch the first exposures, then progress to softened, finely-mashed soya chunks once tolerated. Introduce soy on its own day for attribution.

Context — the formula≠food trap, India, and the myths

⚑ The soy-formula ≠ soy-food trap (read this first). When you see "soy is not recommended for babies," it almost always means soy infant formula as an allergy-prevention strategy — NOT soy foods like tofu. NIAID/ASCIA don't endorse soy formula to prevent allergy; that has nothing to do with feeding a 7-month-old mashed tofu, which is fine. This is the single most common error on this topic. NIAID · ASCIA

Indian & vegetarian context — central, not peripheral

Soya chunks/granules/nuggets (TVP — textured vegetable protein) are everyday, affordable, concentrated plant protein in vegetarian Indian households; tofu and soy milk are increasingly common. In a vegetarian diet, soy is a high-value complete protein — making early, regular introduction both natural and nutritionally useful. Introduce as soft tofu or a minor ingredient first, then work up to softened/mashed soya chunks. staple status

Cross-reactivity to introduce carefully around

Soy ↔ CMPA. Some cow's-milk-protein-allergic babies also react to soy protein. If there's known CMPA, introduce soy cautiously as a minor ingredient first and consider a paediatrician's input. FARE
Legume cross-sensitisation. Soy is a legume, like peanut — some cross-sensitisation potential, though a positive test ≠ clinical allergy. Treat soy as its own allergen, on its own day. FARE

Myths to correct

✓ "Soy is not recommended for babies." Conflation — it's about soy formula as prevention, not tofu/soy foods (fine around 6mo). Highest-value myth on this topic.
✓ "Soy allergy is permanent." Usually false — ~0.4% of infants, commonly outgrown by ~age 3, the majority by age 10.
⚑ "Introduce soy early to prevent soy allergy." The over-claim in the other direction — not proven. EAT found no significant effect for soy. Introduce early because it's safe and nutritious, NOT on a prevention promise.
⚑ "Soy lecithin will trigger a soy-allergic child." Usually not — most soy-allergic individuals tolerate soy lecithin (negligible residual protein); still label-listed, so don't auto-panic on its presence.

Aliases & hidden sources (for the resolver)

Whole-food / fermented: tofu (bean curd), edamame, soybeans, soya chunks/granules/nuggets = TVP, miso, natto, tempeh, soy/soya flour. Liquids & condiments: soy milk/soya drink, soy sauce, tamari (often carry wheat too). Additives: soy lecithin (usually tolerated), soy protein isolate/concentrate.

⚑ Label nuance (US-specific). Highly refined soybean oil is exempt from US major-allergen labelling (protein removed); cold-pressed/unrefined is not. So "no soy listed" on a US label doesn't guarantee soy-oil-free — and "soy lecithin" present doesn't mean a child will react. Applies to US labels, not Indian labelling. Surface TVP/soya chunks as full soy protein; don't auto-escalate lecithin.
