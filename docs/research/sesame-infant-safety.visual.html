Why it matters — a Tier-2 allergen that's usually lifelong

⚑ The honest framing the model must hold: early sesame is SAFE and there's no reason to delay — but RCT evidence does not show early sesame prevents sesame allergy. The EAT trial reported, verbatim: "no significant effects with respect to milk, sesame, fish, or wheat." The peanut+egg benefit does not transfer. Never claim early sesame prevents sesame allergy. EAT verified · PubMed 26943128

Rarely outgrown — the defining fact

~70–80% persists into adulthood — sesame allergy is rarely outgrown.

Only ~20–30% achieve tolerance, unlike egg, milk, soy and wheat (commonly outgrown).

Sesame sits firmly in the lifelong camp with peanut and tree nuts — and is a leading anaphylaxis trigger. J Asthma Allergy · JAMA Netw Open 2019

⚑ Why this drives the tone: for a commonly-outgrown allergen, the risk fades. For sesame it does not — so the emergency floor, label-vigilance, and "carry the adrenaline plan" framing are the most serious of the four, and should not soften over the toddler years.

Introduce early, don't delay AAP names sesame directly

General timing is around 6 months, once ready for solids — not before 4 months, and don't delay once ready.

"There is no evidence that delaying introduction of allergenic foods like egg, peanut, dairy and sesame prevents allergies once babies are ready, typically at about 6 months." — AAP/HealthyChildren

NHS lists sesame among allergens to introduce, noting seeds — "particularly sesame" — should be finely ground. ASCIA recommends common allergens incl. sesame in the first year.

Regulatory weight — the 9th US major allergen

Under the FASTER Act of 2021, sesame became the 9th major US food allergen; mandatory labeling took effect January 1, 2023 (e.g. "tahini (sesame)" or "Contains sesame"). The EU has long required it. FDA page blocked direct fetch — corroborated via FoodSafety.gov mirror

⚑ Scope limit: this labeling is US (and analogously EU) packaged-food regulation. It does NOT apply to Indian-bought packets or home foods — a homemade til laddoo or loose chutney carries no "Contains sesame" guarantee.

Reaction & introduction — the gravest emergency floor

Safe form — the choking gate High

Thinned tahini / ground (SAFE)
introduce early
smooth tahini thinned (~⅛–½ tsp) into milk, water, puree, yoghurt, dal, khichdi or curd; or finely ground sesame stirred in. Same "thin the sticky glob" rule as peanut butter.
Thick glob / whole seeds (CHOKING)
never for a young infant
a thick spoonful of paste (sticky — lodges in the airway) or whole sesame seeds in quantity (small, hard, easily inhaled). Avoid loose til off laddoo/gajak.
✓ Design crux: thinning/grinding genuinely removes the choking hazard (like peanut — unlike honey, where cooking doesn't remove the spore hazard). The allergy axis is separate and NOT removed by form — thinning makes it safe to swallow, not safe for an allergic child.

The reaction watch — most serious of the four

First taste — small amount, at home
~⅛–½ tsp thinned tahini, one allergen at a time, morning/midday so you can observe through the day.
Mild — one body system (watch closely)
Itchy hives/welts, redness or rash, swelling (esp. mouth/eyes), vomiting, sometimes loose stool, sudden fussiness. → stop the food, monitor, call your doctor.
Severe / anaphylaxis — act immediately
Trouble breathing, wheeze/persistent cough, face/lip/tongue/throat swelling, hoarse cry, pale/floppy/lethargic, collapse. In babies, suddenly limp / can't hold head up is a severe sign. → use a prescribed adrenaline auto-injector now, call 112 / 108 (India).
Keep watching — vigilance does NOT age out
First taste may sensitise with the reaction on a later one — watch across the first several exposures. Because sesame is usually permanent, this vigilance doesn't fade the way it tends to for milk/egg.
⚑ Anaphylaxis onset often 5–30 min. Sesame is a leading cause of food-allergic anaphylaxis. Treat first exposures and ongoing care with the most seriousness of the four allergens.

Context — Indian & cultural

Til is deeply embedded in the Indian diet — til laddoo, gajak (sesame-jaggery brittle, peaking around Makar Sankranti, mid-January), sesame chutneys, and gingelly (sesame) oil are everyday foods. Early, regular introduction is culturally natural — the challenge is FORM (thin/grind) and first-exposure discipline, not novelty. cultural embedding

The cultural double-edge

Because til is everywhere and often unlabeled in home/loose form, an Indian family both (a) introduces sesame naturally and early, and (b) faces a harder avoidance task if the child turns out allergic. The US "Contains sesame" label does not appear on a homemade laddoo or a loose chutney. The parent is the label.

Form tip: thin tahini into dal, khichdi, curd or fruit puree; avoid loose til seeds shaken off laddoo/gajak for a young infant — those carry whole seeds and an uncontrolled first-exposure dose.

Hidden under many names

A confirmed-allergic child's caregiver must recognise: tahini, hummus, halva/halwa (sesame variety), til / til laddoo, gajak, benne / benniseed, gomashio, goma, sim sim, and gingelly oil (= sesame oil). Hidden on breads, buns, crackers, bagels, in chutneys, sweets, dressings and spice blends.

⚑ The sesame-oil nuance: highly refined sesame oil is largely de-proteinized and lower-risk — but cold-pressed / unrefined gingelly oil, common in Indian cooking, can retain allergenic protein. For a non-allergic baby this is no barrier; for a known-allergic child, treat unrefined gingelly oil as a potential exposure. Moderate
⚑ Gap — no India-specific directive. No IAP / MoHFW / FSSAI sesame directive (early-intro or labeling) was located. The early-introduction guidance is international (AAP/NHS/ASCIA); the 9th-allergen labeling law is US (FASTER Act) and does not apply in India. Do not launder either onto an Indian body.
